# Building D2: the league, and why it has no opening book

**2026-07-31.** Written the day D2 landed, while `t4h-n64` was still running on the
card. Records the decisions, the two things that were wrong on the way, and the one
number that is a prediction rather than a measurement.

Normative version: `docs/reference/evaluation.md` §5.4. The ledger bullet is in
`docs/ledger/state.md`. This entry is what a reference page cannot say — what was
believed before it was checked.

---

## 1. The question that started it: which book to download

The plan had "openings: a book is acceptable, but each checkpoint must play both
colours from every starting position". The obvious next step was to name a book —
UHO (Sedat Canbaz's unbalanced human openings, distributed by evaluation band), or
a TCEC season book — download it, and write a PGN/EPD reader.

The answer turned out to be **don't**, for three reasons in descending order of
strength.

**The mechanism a UHO book breaks is not our mechanism.** UHO gives one side about
+0.8 pawns so that even a *perfectly played* game is decisive; it exists because
strong engines draw balanced positions. Our draw rate is 87 % because a near-uniform
policy shuffles pieces until threefold repetition fires. A human opening does not
prevent that. Buying a tool for a failure mode we do not have is how a dependency
becomes permanent.

**Games are nearly free, so resolution was never the binding constraint.** The
arithmetic, before writing anything:

```
d = 0.8, 36 games per pairing  ->  ±51 Elo per edge          (§9's closed form)
                     / sqrt(11 edges per node)  ->  ±15 Elo per checkpoint
168 pairings x 36 games x ~100 plies x 64 sims  =  ~39 M evals
```

At the Gate-1a rate that is well under an hour for a 32-checkpoint league. Nothing
here needed a book to reach a usable interval.

**Random openings are, for this league, better.** Eight random legal plies produce
naturally *unbalanced* positions, which means **more** decisive games — the thing
the draw rate is starving us of — and the mandatory colour pairing is exactly what
makes an unbalanced position fair to measure from. A balanced book would push in the
wrong direction. It also gives unlimited diversity, zero dependency, and no
conversation about the tabula rasa boundary at all.

⚠️ The boundary was *not* the reason. `evaluation.md` §2 explicitly permits standard
books in evaluation, since evaluation is measurement. Getting this wrong in the
other direction — refusing a book on purity grounds when the real argument is
technical — would have been just as bad a decision made for a worse reason.

A book earns its dependency at §6, when the scale has to be comparable to a
published one and playing the same openings CCRL plays is the point.

## 2. The thing that made the harness simple

The plan said: *two nets, one batch; evaluate both nets on the whole batch and
select rows by (side to move × colour assignment); 2× encoder cost, immune to parity
desync.*

That is correct and it is what I was about to write. It is also unnecessary.

The two networks alternate by ply. A batch that mixes both colour assignments needs
two evaluations per position — either both nets over the full batch (2×) or a
gather/scatter with dynamic shapes (1× plus churn, since the fused encoder
reallocates its output buffers whenever `n` changes). But the two colour assignments
do not have to share a batch. Play them as **two separate batches**, and inside one
batch every row is at the same ply, so at every moment every row wants the same
network:

- one evaluator call per position, not two — **1× the encoder**;
- static shapes, so no reallocation;
- no row selection logic at all.

The catch is that the lockstep has to *hold*. It does, because every opening is the
same length and because a finished row is restarted **in phase** — a row reset to
the ordinary start position would be a ply out of step with the rest of the batch.
If it ever breaks, half the positions in the batch are evaluated by the wrong
network, the games stay legal, the results stay plausible, and the Elo curve is
quietly meaningless. So `match._swap_evaluator` asserts it every move rather than
documenting it. That is one host synchronisation per move, against a failure that
costs a curve.

This is a deviation from the agreed spec, and it is strictly stronger: the property
the 2× design bought — immunity to parity desync — is now *checked* rather than
sidestepped.

## 3. The oracle that made it testable

A network played against itself scores **exactly** 0.5 per colour-swapped pair,
whatever the games do.

Both halves of a self-match are literally the same game: same opening, same network
on both sides, greedy selection (`eps = 0`, `tau_plies = 0`). So the pair
contributes one point out of two — one to A when A had White and none when A had
Black, or the reverse, or half each on a draw.

It is an identity, not an average. It catches a sign flip, a swapped colour
assignment, and an aggregation that counts one half twice, with `abs=1e-12` and no
sample size to argue about. Every other end-to-end test of a match harness is
statistical and needs hundreds of games; this one needs three.

The whole of `tests/test_league.py` runs on the CPU with stub evaluators, in about
two minutes. That was deliberate: on 2026-07-31 `tests/test_eval.py` shipped a
broken `search_impl` default because every test that drove a real search was
`@pytest.mark.slow` and skipped, so the fast suite stayed green while the path
anybody actually runs raised.

## 4. What was wrong on the way

**The synthetic ladder appeared to prove the fitter compressed the scale.** The
first version of `_simulate` drew a fraction `d` of games as draws and decided the
rest from `Δ`. `fit_elo` then recovered 54 Elo where the truth was 120, and the
obvious conclusion — "Bradley-Terry with half-points compresses by `1−d`" — is a
real and documented effect, so it was believable.

It was the simulator. Drawing first and deciding the rest generates a model whose
expected score is `0.5 + (1−d)(p − 0.5)`, which is **not** the score-based
convention `1/(1 + 10^(−Δ/400))` that `evaluation.md` §9 uses and that `fit_elo`
inverts. The fitter was right and the test was generating a different model. Carving
the draw band symmetrically out of the score fixes it, and the ladder is then
recovered to within 45 Elo at 400 games per edge.

The residue of that half hour is a real thing to know: **our Elo is score-based, not
BayesElo's draw-model Elo.** BayesElo factors draws out and reports a larger number
for the same games. §6's affine map is between our scale and a published one; it is
not a conversion between conventions, and mixing the two would be exactly the
two-scale hazard §6 exists to prevent.

**The interval was going to be wrong by a factor of 2.2 and would have looked
fine.** Plain Bradley-Terry models a game's score as having variance `p(1−p)`. A
match at draw fraction `d` and even strength has variance `(1−d)/4`, which at
`d = 0.8` is five times smaller. So the Laplacian interval is too *wide* by
`1/√(1−d)` ≈ 2.2 — conservative, which is the safe direction, and therefore the kind
of error nobody notices. It is corrected by the standard quasi-likelihood
dispersion, which comes out near `1−d`; `dispersion` and `se_raw` are both reported
so the correction is visible rather than baked in.

## 5. What the first smoke league found

A six-pairing league on the real CUDA path (anchor plus three `t4h-n64`
checkpoints, `--games 4 --sims 8`) ran end to end at the first attempt — pool,
calendar, kernel search, fused encoder, colour pairing, fit, cost join, curve
render. Two things came out of it.

**Every game was a draw at `n = 8`, and the fit reported `±0 Elo`.** That is the
quasi-likelihood correction of §4 breaking down: all draws means zero Pearson
residual, so the estimated dispersion is exactly 0, and scaling the interval by
`√0` gives infinite precision from a sample that says nothing at all about who is
better. It is a false claim of certainty produced by a correction that exists to
remove a false claim of imprecision, which is a pleasing shape for a bug to have.

The guard is `min_decisive`: below 30 decisive games league-wide the dispersion is
not believable, so the uncorrected — conservative, too wide — interval is reported
instead and `dispersion_applied` says so out loud. The same league now reports
±298 to ±342 Elo with a warning, which is the truth about ten games at eight
simulations.

⚠️ Worth naming: this defect would have survived the unit tests. Every synthetic
ladder in `tests/test_league.py` has decisive games in it, because a ladder with no
decisive games has nothing to recover. It took a real league on real checkpoints,
badly parameterised, to produce the degenerate input.

**`load_engine` builds the fused encoder from a CPU fp32 master**, so thirty
checkpoints cost thirty times 13 MB of packed fp16 on the card rather than thirty
times 38 MB with the masters. That was written blind and is now checked against the
torch oracle: policy agrees to 6.2e-3, value to 1.7e-3, both fp16 tolerances, and
the policy comes back fp16 as the kernel's `_expand` requires.

## 6. What is a prediction and not a measurement

⚠️ **No league has been *timed* on the kernel.** The smoke league of §5 ran on it,
but beside a training run using the card at 99 %, so its 6 s per pairing is a number
about contention rather than about the harness. Every throughput figure in this entry
is arithmetic.

The specific prediction worth checking first: the batch is `--games / 2` rows per
half — 18 at the default — and I expect that to be acceptable rather than
catastrophic, **because a board is 32 tokens**, so 18 games is a 576-row GEMM rather
than an 18-row one. If that is wrong, the fix is not more games per pairing (they
cost exactly proportionally); it is refilling finished slots from a queue so several
pairings share a batch, at the price of a row-to-engine map and the gather/scatter
this design just avoided.

The second unquantified cost is the tail: a finished row plays a throwaway game
until the whole batch is done, and with games running 40 to 250 plies that could be
a large fraction of the work. Same seam, same fix.

Predict, measure, explain the gap — the first two of the three are done here and the
third is owed.
